Simulating radiation damage in fusion reactor walls

Volunteers lend spare computer time to simulate what happens when a fast neutron knocks a tungsten atom out of place: a collision cascade lasting a few trillionths of a second. One cascade is routine for any lab. What no single lab can afford is running the same question tens of thousands of times, with several published models of tungsten, to see how much the answers really vary. That is what Nuclear@Home is for.

Why volunteers

How sure are the models?

In one study of three tungsten models (at 100–200 keV), the models agreed on how many defects a cascade leaves but disagreed on the shapes those defects take. Campaign 2 will run the same scenarios (energy, temperature, random directions) with each model, so modellers of reactor walls get honest error bars.

The rare events

Unusually large defect clusters are rare. A hundred simulations can't say how often they form; tens of thousands are needed to measure it. Volume is exactly what thousands of home computers provide.

Open data

Every cascade is checked, run twice on different computers and published with its inputs, so anyone can re-analyse it. Campaign 1 is checking the pipeline against published results first.

What this cannot do

A prediction, not a measurement

No machine on Earth yet makes fusion neutrons at reactor strength, so these results cannot be checked against a real fusion wall. We compare with other published simulations and label every number a model prediction.

Only the first picoseconds

Each work unit follows one neutron hit for about 20 trillionths of a second. Years of damage piling up, heat cycles and the tritium a wall finally holds need other models, which can use our data as input.

Small boxes, pure tungsten

Each simulation must fit on one home computer, which limits the hits we can follow to an estimated 20 keV or so (10 keV is the largest run so far). Real walls are alloys that slowly turn partly into rhenium; we add a material only when a tested model of it exists.

Join Nuclear@Home

Nuclear@Home runs through BOINC, the free volunteer-computing app. Your account, password and team live in BOINC; this site shows the science and the stats.

1. Install BOINC

Linux or Windows on 64-bit Intel or AMD processors (x86_64).

Windows: download BOINC from boinc.berkeley.edu and run the installer.

Ubuntu or Debian:

sudo apt install boinc-client boinc-manager

2. Add the project

In BOINC Manager choose Tools → Add project, enter the project address and create your account (username, email, password) or log in.

The project address will appear here when the invite-only beta opens.

3. Teams

Create or join a team from your account page on the project site. Your points then count for the team too.

Team join codes, approved by team managers, are coming with the beta.

Choose how your computer helps

These are BOINC Manager settings (Options → Computing preferences); they apply to every BOINC project.

You wantSetting

Use only the GPUOn the project site: Your account → Preferences for this project → untick "Use CPU"

Every finished work unit is uploaded automatically and checked by the server before it earns points. Your computer keeps no copies; the latest cascade of each computer can be viewed in 3D on the Machines and Work Units pages.
